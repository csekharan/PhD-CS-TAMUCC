Doctor of Philosophy in Computer Science

A research degree in computer science, with an optional specialization in geospatial informatics. Students enter from a bachelor's or a master's degree, complete a common core, and spend most of the program carrying out original research with a faculty advisor.

Overview

The PhD in Computer Science prepares students to carry out independent research and to communicate it in the forms the field expects: peer-reviewed papers, conference presentations, and a dissertation defended before a faculty committee. The degree certifies both a broad command of the discipline, demonstrated through a common core and a candidacy examination, and a substantial original contribution to it.

The program is advisor centered and anchored on student excellence and success. Admitted students have an opportunity to join a research group on arrival by contacting them early. Students entering with a master's degree typically finish in about four years; those entering with a bachelor's degree usually need five years.

Faculty research is concentrated in artificial intelligence and machine learning, cybersecurity, wireless networking and high performance computing, autonomous systems and computer vision, algorithms and applications, and geospatial informatics. Much of it draws on the university's setting on the Texas Gulf Coast: the Port of Corpus Christi, the region's energy and pipeline industry, the Autonomy Research Institute (ARI), and the Conrad Blucher Institute for Surveying and Science are all on or near campus.

Graduates of the program hold faculty, postdoctoral, and industry positions; they are listed under Recent PhD Graduates.

Research Areas and Faculty

Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

Guo Cheng, Taoran Ji, Scott King, Chandra N. Sekharan, Wenlu Wang

Large language models and agentic systems, time-series forecasting and event detection, natural language processing, deep sequential modeling, AI for science, and efficient, trustworthy vision-language models.

Cybersecurity

Dulal Kar, Bozhen Liu, Carlos Rubio-Medrano, Mehdi Sookhak

Security of cyber-physical and energy delivery systems, software verification and program analysis for security, network and information security, blockchain, and cloud and IoT security.

Wireless Networking and High Performance Computing

Dulal Kar, Bozhen Liu, Chandra N. Sekharan, Mehdi Sookhak

Wireless sensor and low-power wide-area networks, cellular and vehicular networks, cloud, fog, and edge computing, and concurrent, parallel, and distributed systems.

Autonomous Systems and Computer Vision

Guo Cheng, Scott King, Harish Ram Nambiappan, Michael Starek

Streaming video understanding for autonomous vehicles, lightweight perception at the edge, assistive robotics and human-robot interaction, autonomous mapping with unmanned aircraft, and visualization.

Algorithms and Applications

Dulal Kar, Chandra N. Sekharan

Design and analysis of algorithms, graph algorithms and social networks, and data compression.

Geospatial Informatics

Tianxing Chu, Lucy Huang, Michael Starek

Lidar and geospatial remote sensing, 3D reality capture with unmanned aircraft, navigation, positioning, and indoor SLAM, ontologies and semantic interoperability for spatial data, geospatial data science, and coastal hazards. Offered with the Conrad Blucher Institute.

Identifying an advisor. Applicants are expected to identify a prospective advisor before applying. Read the research summaries above, then write only to faculty members whose work matches your interests. Include a CV and a short description of the problem you would like to work on. If you are unsure whom to contact, the PhD Program Coordinator can suggest faculty based on your background.

Admissions

To learn about the application process, visit Application Process.

Eligibility

Applicants hold, or will hold before enrolling, a bachelor's or master's degree in computer science or a closely related field from a regionally accredited institution, or the equivalent from an institution outside the United States. Applicants from adjacent fields are considered case by case. Applicants to the geospatial informatics specialization should have a strong foundation in GIS, geomatics, or geospatial data analysis.

Financial support

A limited number of research and teaching assistantships are available each year. Research assistantships are funded from faculty grants and place the student on the project their dissertation grows out of; many students in the program are supported this way. Teaching assistantships support undergraduate instruction in the department and carry a half-time appointment. Support depends on the advisor, project availability, and the student's qualifications, so it is arranged with the prospective advisor before admission and renewed each year while the student makes satisfactory progress.

The department also offers Graduate Research Assistantships for highly qualified students. All doctoral students are eligible for receiving limited travel support for presenting their research work at conferences.

Degree requirements

The program has two entry points with a common structure. Students entering with a master's degree may have core courses validated against their prior transcript and replaced with electives or research. Full requirements are in the graduate catalog.

Entering with a bachelor's degree: 75 credit hours
Core: algorithms, computer architecture, operating systems, software engineering12
Graduate seminar, taken in the first year3
Electives from computer science and geospatial graduate courses30
Research, dissertation research, and dissertation defense30
Entering with a master's degree: 57 credit hours
Core: algorithms, computer architecture, operating systems, software engineering12
Graduate seminar, taken in the first year3
Electives (minimum; more when core courses are validated)12
Research, dissertation research, and dissertation defense30

The geospatial informatics specialization adds two courses from geospatial data structures, advanced geospatial computing, and spatial systems science, and requires that a portion of electives come from geospatial courses.

Milestones

  1. First yearDegree plan and seminarJoin the advisor's group, take the graduate seminar on research methods and writing, and file a degree plan approved by the advisory committee by the end of the second semester.
  2. Years 1 to 2Core coursesComplete the four core areas, or validate them from a prior transcript, and begin research hours.
  3. Year 2 to 3 confirmCandidacy examinationDemonstrate command of the core and the readiness of the dissertation prospectus before the committee.
  4. Years 3 to 4Dissertation researchResearch under the advisor's supervision, with publication, conference travel, and an annual progress review.
  5. Final semesterDissertation defenseA public defense before the full advisory committee, followed by submission of the approved dissertation.
